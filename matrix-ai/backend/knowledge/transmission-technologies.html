Definition

Internet access technologies are the physical and logical means by which an access provider like Matrix Telecom connects its customers to the global network. Each technology has specific characteristics in terms of speed, distance, cost, and reliability. The choice of technology depends on the customer's location, their needs, and the existing infrastructure.

xDSL (ADSL, VDSL)

xDSL uses the copper pairs of the existing telephone network. A splitter separates voice and data. ADSL (Asymmetric Digital Subscriber Line) offers asymmetric speeds: up to 20 Mb/s downstream and 1 Mb/s upstream. The maximum distance is about 5 km from the central office. VDSL (Very-high-bit-rate DSL) reaches 100 Mb/s downstream but over less than 1.2 km.

How It Works

The DSL signal uses higher frequencies than voice (25 kHz to 1 MHz for ADSL, up to 30 MHz for VDSL). The DSL modem modulates data onto these frequencies. Attenuation increases with distance and frequency, limiting speed. Noise (crosstalk) between cable pairs also affects performance.

Fiber Optic (FTTH)

Fiber optic uses light to transmit data through a glass or plastic cable. Speeds can reach several gigabits per second over distances of 20 km and more. It is the preferred technology for Matrix Telecom for new installations. See the dedicated « Fiber Optic » page for details.

LTE / 4G / 5G

Mobile networks are used by Matrix Telecom as a backup solution or for temporary customers. A 4G antenna can cover up to 10 km in rural areas. 5G offers much higher speeds but shorter range (1-2 km for high bands). Matrix uses 4G/5G routers with external antennas to improve reception.

Frequencies Used

  • 4G LTE: 800 MHz, 1800 MHz, 2600 MHz
  • 5G NR: 3.5 GHz (mid-band), 26 GHz (high-band)
  • The lower the frequency, the greater the range and wall penetration.

Microwave

Microwave links are used to connect sites without fiber infrastructure. They require Line of Sight (LoS) between the two antennas. Typical frequencies are 5 GHz, 11 GHz, 23 GHz. Range can reach 50+ km with parabolic antennas. Matrix uses microwave links to connect its POPs together.

How It Works

A radio transmitter produces a high-frequency signal focused by a directional antenna toward the receiving antenna. The transmission zone (Fresnel) must be clear of obstacles. The free space path loss formula is: FSPL = 32.45 + 20×log10(d) + 20×log10(f) where d is distance in km and f is frequency in MHz.

Satellite

Matrix Telecom uses satellite as a last resort solution for customers in extremely remote areas. New generations of LEO satellites (Starlink, OneWeb) offer latencies of 20-40 ms and speeds of 100 to 500 Mb/s, making satellite viable for some professional applications.

Practical Case at Matrix Telecom Yaoundé

Choosing the Right Technology According to the Customer

Scenario 1: A professional customer in the Bastos neighborhood (business district) needs a reliable connection with 100 Mb/s symmetric, guaranteed SLA.

Solution: Fiber optic FTTH. Matrix deploys a cable from the nearest ODF cabinet (usually less than 500 m away). Installation of a Gigabit ONT. SLA 99.9%.

Scenario 2: A residential customer in a peripheral neighborhood (e.g., Nkolbisson) without fiber.

Solution: Ubiquiti NanoStation point-to-point link from a POP located on a nearby hill. If direct line of sight is not possible, 4G with external antenna is offered.

Scenario 3: Connect two Matrix offices 8 km apart, with no possibility of running fiber.

Solution: MikroTik or Mimosa microwave link with 60 cm parabolic antennas, 5 GHz frequency, actual throughput 300 Mb/s.

Scenario 4: Temporary customer (construction site, event) for 3 months.

Solution: 4G router with external antenna and data plan. Installation in 1 day, no long-term commitment.

Tools Used

Spectrum Analyzer
Usage:
  • Check radio interference in a frequency band before installing a WiFi or microwave link
  • Identify the least used channels (5 GHz, 2.4 GHz)
  • Detect noise sources (microwaves, other APs)
  • Models: Rohde & Schwarz FSH, Anritsu Spectrum Master, or WiFi Analyzer app on smartphone
Signal Meter (RSSI Meter)
Usage:
  • Measure received signal strength (RSSI) in dBm
  • Align antennas to maximize signal
  • Verify link quality before validation
  • Typical threshold: good signal > -60 dBm, acceptable > -75 dBm
  • Tools: airOS (Ubiquiti), WinBox (MikroTik), WiFi Man app
Site Survey Tools
Usage:
  • Ekahau HeatMapper: WiFi mapping, coverage visualization
  • NetSpot: WiFi analysis, dead zone identification
  • Google Earth: LoS verification, distance measurement
  • Link.ubnt.com: Ubiquiti link simulator (enter coordinates of both points)

Example Command: Link Verification with Ping

# Test latency and packet loss on a link
ping -n 100 10.0.0.1

# Measure throughput with iperf3
iperf3 -c 10.0.0.1 -t 30 -i 5

# Check signal quality on a NanoStation (SSH)
ssh admin@192.168.1.20
# In airOS: check the Main tab - Signal Strength

# Trace route to a client
tracert -d 8.8.8.8

Tip for the Intern

Tip:
  • Do not rely solely on theoretical speeds. Always test actual throughput with iperf3 or speedtest.net.
  • For a radio link, verifying direct Line of Sight (LoS) is crucial. A tree or building can block everything.
  • Document each installation with: technology used, GPS coordinates, signal strength, date.
  • Know the legal frequencies: in Cameroon, ART (Telecommunications Regulatory Agency) regulates frequency usage.
  • Always have a backup plan: if fiber is cut, offer 4G as backup.
  • For demanding customers, prioritize fiber whenever possible. It is the most sustainable investment.
  • Learn to read performance indicators: SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio), RSSI, CINR, Latency, Jitter, Packet Loss.
