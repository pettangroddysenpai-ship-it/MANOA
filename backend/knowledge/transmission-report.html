1. GPS & Google Earth for the Network

1.1 Introduction

GPS (Global Positioning System) is a satellite navigation system that precisely determines the geographic coordinates of a point on Earth. It uses a constellation of 31 satellites orbiting at approximately 20,200 km altitude, each continuously emitting a signal containing its position and precise time via atomic clocks. A GPS receiver calculates its distance from at least 4 satellites by measuring signal propagation time, then determines its 3D position (latitude, longitude, altitude) with 2 to 5 meter accuracy under normal conditions.

Google Earth is a virtual globe application that uses satellite imagery, aerial photography, and GIS (Geographic Information System) data to create a 3D model of the Earth. KML/KMZ files allow importing points, lines, and polygons to overlay network information on the map. At Matrix Telecom, GPS and Google Earth are used for tower positioning, fiber optic route mapping, customer cartography, and network deployment optimization in the neighborhoods of Yaoundé.

1.3 Research Summary

How It Works

GPS operates on the principle of trilateration. Each satellite transmits a signal containing its orbital position (ephemeris) and the exact transmission time. The receiver compares the transmission time to its own internal clock to calculate the distance to each satellite. With signals from at least four satellites, the receiver can solve for four unknowns: latitude, longitude, altitude, and time offset. Accuracy is influenced by satellite geometry (DOP — Dilution of Precision), atmospheric conditions, and multipath effects from buildings.

Coordinate Systems

Latitude and Longitude: Latitude measures north-south position relative to the equator (0° to 90° N/S). Longitude measures east-west position relative to the Greenwich meridian (0° to 180° E/W). For Yaoundé, the approximate coordinates are 3°52′N 11°31′E.

UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator): Divides the Earth into 60 zones of 6° longitude each. Yaoundé is in UTM zone 33N. UTM coordinates are expressed in meters (Easting, Northing), facilitating distance and area calculations.

Coordinate Formats:

  • DD (Decimal Degrees): 3.8667°N, 11.5167°E — ideal for Google Earth
  • DMS (Degrees Minutes Seconds): 3°52′00″N, 11°31′00″E — traditional format
  • UTM: 33N 332500E 4275000N — used for field surveys

Coordinate Conversion DMS → DD

Formula: DD = Degrees + (Minutes / 60) + (Seconds / 3600)

Yaoundé: 3°52'00"N 11°31'00"E
Latitude DD = 3 + 52/60 + 00/3600 = 3.8667°N
Longitude DD = 11 + 31/60 + 00/3600 = 11.5167°E

Distance Calculation (Haversine Formula)

d = 2 · R · arcsin(√(sin²(Δlat/2) + cos(lat1) · cos(lat2) · sin²(Δlon/2)))
Where R = 6371 km (Earth radius)

1.6 Problems Encountered and Solutions

Problem 1: Understanding GPS Accuracy and HDOP

I initially did not understand why GPS readings varied when standing in the same location. The concept of HDOP (Horizontal Dilution of Precision) was unclear. I thought a GPS fix was simply "on" or "off."

Solution:

Researching Dilution of Precision revealed that satellite geometry directly affects accuracy. When satellites are clustered together (poor geometry), HDOP is high and accuracy degrades. When they are spread across the sky, HDOP is low. Using a GPS test app on my smartphone, I observed real-time HDOP values and satellite positions. I learned that an HDOP < 1 is excellent, < 2 is good, and > 5 is poor. Waiting for more satellites (at least 6) and better geometry before recording coordinates became standard practice. This explained the variation I observed.

Problem 2: Confusion Between Coordinate Formats

I initially mixed up Decimal Degrees (DD) and Degrees Minutes Seconds (DMS), entering coordinates in the wrong format into Google Earth. For example, entering 3.8667 as if it were DMS (3°86′) produced a location in the wrong continent.

Solution:

I created a reference card comparing the three formats (DD, DMS, UTM) with conversion formulas. Practicing conversions between formats using the formula DD = Degrees + Minutes/60 + Seconds/3600 helped internalize the relationship. I also learned that Google Earth accepts coordinates in DD format directly in the search bar, while handheld GPS devices typically display DMS. Knowing which format each tool expects eliminated the entry errors.

Problem 3: Understanding the Haversine Formula

The Haversine formula with trigonometric functions seemed complex, and I struggled to see why a simple Euclidean distance wouldn't work on a spherical Earth.

Solution:

Studying the derivation showed that on a sphere, the straight-line distance through the Earth (Euclidean) differs from the great-circle distance along the surface. The Haversine formula accounts for Earth's curvature. I worked through the calculation for two points in Yaoundé step by step on paper, then verified the result using Google Earth's ruler tool. The calculated distance of 8.47 km between Bastos and Mvan matched Google Earth's measurement, confirming my understanding.

The ability to collect precise waypoints, create organized KML maps, and measure distances and elevation profiles enables Matrix technicians to plan fiber routes, position antennas, and document the network infrastructure systematically. 2. Internet Access Technologies

2.1 Introduction

Internet access technologies are the physical and logical means by which an access provider like Matrix Telecom connects its customers to the global network. Each technology has specific characteristics in terms of speed, distance, cost, and reliability. The choice of technology depends on the customer's location, their needs, and the existing infrastructure.

Matrix Telecom operates in the Yaoundé metropolitan area where different zones have different infrastructure readiness. Business districts like Bastos have extensive fiber coverage, while peripheral neighborhoods like Nkolbisson rely on wireless solutions. Understanding the full range of access technologies — from xDSL and fiber to 4G/5G, microwave, satellite, and WiFi point-to-point — allows Matrix engineers to recommend the optimal solution for each client context.

xDSL (ADSL, VDSL)

xDSL uses the copper pairs of the existing telephone network. The DSL signal uses higher frequencies than voice (25 kHz to 1 MHz for ADSL, up to 30 MHz for VDSL). The DSL modem modulates data onto these frequencies. Attenuation increases with distance and frequency, limiting speed. VDSL reaches 100 Mb/s downstream but over less than 1.2 km from the central office.

LTE / 4G / 5G

Mobile networks use frequency bands: 4G LTE at 800 MHz, 1800 MHz, 2600 MHz; 5G NR at 3.5 GHz (mid-band) and 26 GHz (high-band). The lower the frequency, the greater the range and wall penetration. Matrix uses 4G/5G routers with external antennas as backup or temporary solutions.

Microwave

Microwave links connect sites without fiber infrastructure and require Line of Sight (LoS). Typical frequencies are 5 GHz, 11 GHz, 23 GHz. The Free Space Path Loss formula is: FSPL = 32.45 + 20×log10(d) + 20×log10(f) where d is distance in km and f is frequency in MHz.

2.6 Problems Encountered and Solutions

Problem 1: Confusing Technology Specifications

Initially, I memorized the theoretical speeds of each technology without understanding the practical limitations. For example, I thought ADSL could deliver 20 Mb/s at any distance under 5 km, not realizing that speed drops significantly with distance.

Solution:

I studied the relationship between signal attenuation and distance for each technology. Drawing attenuation curves showed that ADSL speed drops exponentially beyond 2 km, and VDSL is only viable within 1.2 km. For wireless technologies, I learned that the Free Space Path Loss increases with both distance and frequency. This understanding shifted my focus from theoretical maximums to realistic throughput given the specific deployment scenario. I also learned to account for environmental factors like rain fade on microwave links and building penetration loss on 4G/5G.

Problem 2: Line of Sight Assessment

I underestimated the importance of Line of Sight for wireless links. I assumed that if two points were close enough, a radio link would work. I did not consider obstacles like trees, buildings, or terrain elevation.

Solution:

Using Google Earth's elevation profile tool, I learned to draw a path between two candidate points and examine the terrain along the route. I also studied the Fresnel zone concept — the elliptical area around the direct LoS that must be clear of obstacles. The rule is that 60% of the Fresnel zone radius must be clear for reliable operation. Using online tools like Link.ubnt.com and RF calculators, I practiced determining whether a link was feasible given specific coordinates and antenna heights.

Problem 3: QoS Configuration for Mixed Traffic

I initially thought QoS was simply about setting bandwidth limits. I did not understand how to prioritize real-time traffic (VoIP, video) over bulk data (downloads) on a single link.

Solution:

Studying MikroTik RouterOS queue trees and firewall mangle rules clarified how packet marking works. I learned to identify traffic by protocol (UDP/TCP), port ranges, and DSCP markings, then assign priorities using PCQ and HTB queue types. Creating a lab setup with two clients and bandwidth contention allowed me to test that VoIP traffic (DSCP EF) was prioritized over HTTP downloads during congestion. The key insight was that QoS does not create bandwidth — it ensures important traffic gets through first when bandwidth is limited.

3. Fiber Optic — FTTH

3.1 Introduction

Fiber optic is a transmission medium that uses light (laser or LED) to carry data at very high speeds over long distances. It exploits the principle of total internal reflection: the glass core (silica) has a higher refractive index than the optical cladding, so when light is injected at an angle less than the critical angle, it remains trapped inside and propagates through successive reflections.

At Matrix Telecom, fiber optic is the flagship technology for FTTH (Fiber To The Home) connections deployed in the neighborhoods of Yaoundé. It offers symmetric speeds up to 1 Gb/s with unmatched reliability and low latency (< 5 ms). The FTTH network uses GPON (Gigabit Passive Optical Network) technology where a single fiber from the OLT serves up to 64 or 128 subscribers through passive optical splitters, making it cost-effective for dense urban deployments.

Components of an FTTH Network

OLT (Optical Line Terminal): Active equipment at Matrix's POP. Central point of GPON, managing up to 64 or 128 ONU/ONT. Emits laser at 1490 nm downstream, receives at 1310 nm upstream.

ONU / ONT (Optical Network Unit / Terminal): Customer premises equipment converting optical to electrical (Ethernet). Common models: Huawei HG8245, Nokia G-240W-A, Ubiquiti UFiber.

GPON (Gigabit Passive Optical Network): Point-to-multipoint using passive splitters. Typical distribution: Splitter 1:4 at POP (1st stage), Splitter 1:8 or 1:16 at distribution point (2nd stage). Total 1:32 or 1:64 per OLT port. Bandwidth: 2.5 Gb/s downstream, 1.25 Gb/s upstream (shared).

ODF (Optical Distribution Frame): Patching cabinet at POP and distribution points connecting fibers from customers to OLT ports via pigtails and SC/APC or SC/UPC adapters.

How Light Transmission Works

Wavelengths used:

  • 1310 nm: upstream (client → POP), attenuation ~0.35 dB/km
  • 1490 nm: downstream (POP → client), attenuation ~0.25 dB/km
  • 1550 nm: long distance links, attenuation ~0.20 dB/km

Complete FTTH Installation Process

  1. Planning: Identify neighborhood, map infrastructure, size OLT ports, choose ODF cabinet locations
  2. Backbone Deployment: Run 12-144 fiber cables (G.652D) from POP to distribution points, fusion splice, test with OTDR
  3. Distribution Network: Install splitters (1:8 or 1:16) in ODF cabinets, run drop cables to homes
  4. Customer Installation: Position fiber entry, splice SC/APC pigtail, test with VFL, connect ONT, configure WiFi router
  5. Service Activation: ONT registers with OLT via serial number/LOID, DBA assigns bandwidth, traffic in GPON GEM frames

3.6 Problems Encountered and Solutions

Problem 1: Understanding GPON Architecture

I initially had difficulty understanding how a single fiber could serve multiple subscribers simultaneously. The concept of passive optical splitters and the distinction between downstream broadcast and upstream TDMA was confusing.

Solution:

Creating a diagram of the GPON network helped visualize the architecture. Downstream (1490 nm) is broadcast: the OLT sends data in 125 µs frames, and each ONT reads only its assigned timeslots. Upstream (1310 nm) uses Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA): the OLT assigns transmission windows to each ONT to prevent collisions. The splitter is completely passive and simply divides the optical power. I tested this understanding by tracing the path of a single packet from the Internet through the OLT, splitter, and ONT to a customer's laptop, identifying the protocol state at each step.

Problem 2: Optical Power Budget Calculation

I found the power budget calculation overwhelming with multiple loss elements. I was not sure how to account for connector losses, splice losses, and splitter losses together, and whether the result was acceptable.

Solution:

I broke the calculation into a systematic table with four columns: element, loss per unit, quantity, and subtotal. By calculating each element separately and summing them, the total loss became transparent. I then compared the result to the available budget (OLT transmit power minus ONT sensitivity). Working through the Mvan deployment example — where the farthest customer had 18 dB total loss against a 32 dB budget — confirmed that the methodology produces real-world results. I also learned that a negative remaining margin means the link will not work and requires redesign (reduce distance, use lower-ratio splitters, or choose higher-power optics).

Problem 3: Reading OTDR Traces

The OTDR trace showed peaks and steps that I could not interpret. I did not know how to distinguish a connector reflection from a fiber break, or a splice from a micro-bend.

Solution:

Studying the physics of OTDR operation clarified the trace elements. The OTDR sends a laser pulse and measures backscattered light over time. A reflective event (sharp peak) occurs at connectors and mechanical splices where the refractive index changes abruptly. A non-reflective event (step loss) occurs at fusion splices where the change is gradual. The fiber end appears as a large reflection followed by noise. I practiced by comparing OTDR traces from known-good and known-bad fibers in the lab, identifying each event type. I learned that the launch cable is essential to measure the first connector correctly, and that the event dead zone limits how close two events can be distinguished.

Problem 4: Handling and Cleaning Fibers

Initially, I handled fiber connectors casually, not realizing how sensitive they are to contamination. After testing a patch cord that showed 3 dB loss, I assumed the equipment was faulty.

Solution:

I learned that a single dust particle on a fiber core can cause 1-5 dB of loss or even damage the connector end-face. Using a fiber inspection microscope revealed visible contamination on the connector. Cleaning with isopropyl alcohol and lint-free wipes, or using a click-cleaner, restored the loss to 0.2 dB. I adopted the practice of always inspecting and cleaning connectors before mating them, and capping unused connectors immediately. This reduced measurement errors and equipment damage significantly.

The FTTH architecture — from the OLT at the POP through passive splitters to the ONT at the customer premises — delivers reliable, high-bandwidth connectivity that no copper-based technology can match. Mastering the optical power budget calculation, OTDR trace analysis, fusion splicing technique, and proper connector handling provides the practical skills needed for FTTH deployment and maintenance. The GPON standard's efficiency in serving multiple subscribers over a single fiber makes fiber deployment economically viable in dense urban areas like the neighborhoods of Yaoundé.

Overall Conclusion

This research has provided a comprehensive theoretical and practical foundation for three fundamental transmission concepts at Matrix Telecom: GPS & Google Earth for network mapping, Internet access technologies for customer connectivity, and Fiber Optic FTTH for high-speed broadband deployment.

GPS and Google Earth form the spatial intelligence layer of Matrix's operations. The ability to precisely locate infrastructure, measure distances, verify line of sight, and create shareable KML maps enables efficient network planning and documentation. Coordinate system literacy and accuracy awareness prevent costly field errors.

Internet Access Technologies represent the toolbox from which Matrix engineers select the right solution for each client. The comparison of fiber, wireless, mobile, and satellite technologies — each with its speed, range, cost, and reliability profile — provides a structured decision framework. Practical skills in throughput testing, LoS verification, and QoS configuration transform technology knowledge into reliable service delivery.

Fiber Optic FTTH is the premium technology that powers Matrix's growth in urban areas. From the physics of total internal reflection to the practicalities of fusion splicing and OTDR testing, the complete FTTH workflow — planning, backbone deployment, distribution, customer installation, and service activation — represents the end-to-end engineering process that delivers gigabit connectivity to subscribers.

Together, these three concepts form a coherent body of knowledge: GPS tells us where to build, access technologies tell us how to connect, and fiber tells us how to build it best. Mastery of these foundations equips any engineer to contribute effectively to Matrix Telecom's mission of providing reliable, high-quality Internet services across Cameroon.
